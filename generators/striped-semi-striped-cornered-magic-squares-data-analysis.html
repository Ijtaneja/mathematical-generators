Striped and Semi-Striped Cornered Magic Squares

Detailed data analysis of an expanding upper-left corner, equal-sum rectangular regions, and the completed magic square. Includes twelve numbered examples, graphs, and an explicit check of an order-10 exception.

1. Scope of the current generator

The supplied generator stores one square for each order from 5 to 108. Its odd and even constructions begin with different upper-left corner regions, then add right-side and lower strips. The complete n×n square uses each integer from 1 to n² once and has the usual magic sum.

104verified full squares, one per order
52 + 52odd and even orders
5,116marked internal regions checked
5,114regions with equal internal rows and columns
For order n, mean entry μn = (n² + 1) / 2 and full magic sum Mn = n(n² + 1) / 2 = nμn.

The term cornered describes the placement of the starting region in the upper-left. The adjoining strips expand the square simultaneously to the right and below. The displayed 3×3 odd core has equal row and column sums but unequal diagonal sums; it is a semi-magic square, not itself a magic square.

For a marked h×w region with balanced mean μn, the expected equal internal row and column sums are wμn and hμn. A rectangular region has different row and column sums whenever h differs from w. The order-10 exception below shows why these values must be checked rather than assumed.

Example, order 7: μ₇ = 25 and M₇ = 175. The upper-left 3×3 rows and columns sum to 75; its adjacent 3×2 has internal row sum 50 and column sum 75; the 2×7 lower strip has row sum 175 and column sum 50.

5. Reading the four larger examples

The larger grids show the recurring pattern more clearly. Each right-side piece meets a lower piece of the next width. The same local arithmetic is repeated while the full diagonal check remains an independent requirement.

Order 13: five right-and-bottom expansions take the 3×3 corner to order 13. Its last 2×13 band has two rows of sum 1,105. The 3×3 corner remains semi-magic even though the completed 13×13 has two correct long diagonals.

Order 14: the upper-left 4×4 is split into two 2×4 rectangles. A 12×2 right strip and a 2×14 bottom strip finish the construction. Because μ₁₄ is a half-integer, a 2×w row sum is w×98.5; the even widths keep all sums integral.

Order 15: the first diagonal of the 3×3 core sums to 672 while its rows, columns, and other diagonal sum to 339. This particularly strong contrast illustrates why the core must be described as semi-magic. The full 15×15 square still has both long diagonals at 1,695.

Order 16: the multiple-of-four procedure groups the additions in pairs. At the final stage, two 12×2 right rectangles have row sum 257 and column sum 1,542; two 2×16 lower rectangles each have row sum 2,056 and column sum 257. All four are balanced magic rectangles.

7. Independent verification and mathematical limits

  1. For each of the 104 stored orders, check that the grid is n×n and its sorted entries are precisely 1,…,n².
  2. Check all n complete rows, all n complete columns, and both complete diagonals against Mn.
  3. Reconstruct the generator’s marked regions and check that they cover every cell once with no gaps or overlap.
  4. Compute each region’s individual row and column sums. For square-shaped internal regions, also compute both diagonals; report them separately from the full-square diagonals.

Result: 104/104 complete squares pass; 5,116/5,116 region partitions cover their grids; 5,114/5,116 regions have equal internal rows and columns. Only the bottom 2×6 and right 6×2 at order 10 fail that internal test. None of the 52 odd upper-left 3×3 cores is a fully magic 3×3.

This is a numerical audit of the current generator data and its stated partitions. It is not a proof that the same construction works for every larger order or for different values substituted into the stored grids.
